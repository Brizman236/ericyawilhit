The Cotonou Declaration Left Something Out...

African ministers signed the Cotonou Declaration with immense digital ambitions. But a fundamental infrastructure was forgotten: the PKI.
March 17, 2026 | Translations:
  • Fr

Authority Vs Anarchy#

A person claiming to be a police officer stops you while you are in your car and asks for your papers. How would you recognize them as a police officer? Why, even if they are one, would they have the authority to ask you for your papers? What legitimizes them?

First of all, what would allow you to distinguish them as a police officer would be the unique attire worn by the police officers in your city. Being a police officer, they have been mandated by the State to verify papers; the State has given them the power to do so.

Who, however, is the State to be able to mandate an individual and confer this or that right upon them? Where does this power come from? This authority?

The answer is simple: the people, you, have recognized it as an authority capable of granting this or that power to this or that entity.

We accept the authority of the police officer because the State, an entity we trust and recognize as an authority, has granted them this power.

Now, what would happen if this source of trust did not exist? If no one recognized anyone?

Anarchy. We would not accept the authority of the police officer, not only because we would not recognize this authority in them, but also because no entity recognized by us as an authority would have granted them such power.

A law established by the government would then not be recognized because the government itself would not be recognized as having the authority to sign laws and enforce them.

Without authority, anarchy sets in.

An Internet Without Law#

On the Internet, multiple entities or systems are interconnected and exchange information and resources. The question we might ask ourselves is how these entities or systems manage to trust one another. Is there a certain third-party entity recognized by all systems to certify their identity?

A minister performs a transaction between two banks in two different African countries. There is no common trusted authority between these two systems.

Since the two systems cannot trust each other due to this lack of authority, it would be possible for the minister to make a transaction to a malicious actor, as the bank would be unable to verify the recipient’s identity.

Without an authority to allow systems to trust one another, the Internet would be a city without law.

And yet, Africa is currently building this city.

The Cotonou Declaration#

In November 2025, digital ministers from West and Central Africa signed the Cotonou Declaration during the Regional Summit on Digital Transformation.

To accelerate digital transformation, clear targets were set to be achieved by 2030: establishment of an African digital market, digital identity for at least 50% of the population, and integration of interoperable payment systems between multiple countries (at least 15).

These ambitious goals rely on something that was not mentioned: trust between digital systems.

This trust does not establish itself spontaneously between systems; it relies on a precise infrastructure: the PKI.

The only thing that could make all the planned actions possible was not mentioned a single time.

Without PKI, It Won’t Work#

In the digital world, on the Internet, a certain trust exists between systems—a mechanism allowing them to identify and recognize each other.

When you type google.com into your browser, what tells you that the site you reach is truly Google’s? How can you be sure? How does your browser recognize whether a site is genuinely the one it claims to be?

On the Internet, a certain entity exists that makes it possible to certify the identity of systems. However, who is this entity to have such power? Who gave it to them?

This entity has been recognized by both systems (the browser and Google) as an authority possessing the power to certify the identity of one or the other.

The same principle applies to all interactions between different systems on the Internet: an entity recognized as a trusted authority must exist there.

All of this mechanism relies on PKI.

It would be impossible to design interoperable infrastructures between different African countries or a single African market if a mechanism allowing systems to trust one another did not exist.

African digital sovereignty will not be built without a trusted infrastructure. Let us invest in it.
